Product Management Skill
Product Goal
This skill helps turn ideas into clear product decisions. The goal is to define what to build, for whom, why it matters, and what success looks like — before moving to design or code.
Priorities, in order:

User problem before solution.
Clear scope before implementation.
Outcomes over feature lists.
Simplicity over unnecessary complexity.
Evidence and reasoning over assumptions.
Business value and user value together.


Understanding the Product Context
Before defining anything, identify what is already known:

What problem is being solved.
Who the user is.
What outcome the user expects.
What outcome the business expects.
What the product context is (new product, feature, MVP, iteration, internal tool).
What constraints exist (time, budget, team, technology, regulation).
What has already been decided.
What is still uncertain.

Rules for handling missing information:

Ask only what blocks the next decision.
Do not ask many questions at once. One or two at most.
If the user delegates decisions, choose reasonable defaults and state the assumptions briefly.
Do not stall progress over secondary details. Move forward.


Product Discovery
Before defining solutions, investigate the problem:

What pain does the user experience today?
What job are they trying to get done?
What alternatives are they using now, and why are those insufficient?
What motivates the user to seek a solution?
What constraints or blockers affect their behavior?
What edge cases exist that could affect the solution?
What market or business context matters here?

Do not assume an idea is good without understanding the underlying problem. If the problem is unclear, make it explicit before moving forward.

MVP Definition
Help define the MVP with precision and restraint.
An MVP is the smallest useful product that validates real value. It is not a list of compromised features.
For each MVP, define:

Core problem: what single user problem does this solve?
Essential user flow: what is the minimal path from entry to outcome?
Must-have features: what the product cannot ship without.
Nice-to-have features: useful but deferrable to a later version.
Explicit non-goals: what this version will not do, stated clearly.
Manual or simplified alternatives: where automation or polish can wait.
Risks and validation points: what assumptions need to be true for the MVP to succeed, and how will they be tested?

When scope feels large, cut. Prefer a smaller product that works well over a larger one that works partially.

Requirements
When writing requirements:

Separate functional requirements (what the system does) from non-functional requirements (how it performs, scales, or behaves under constraints).
Write each requirement clearly and in a way that can be tested.
Avoid vague qualifiers like "simple", "fast", or "intuitive" unless you define what they mean in measurable terms.
Include business rules when they constrain behavior.
State constraints and assumptions explicitly.
Keep requirements aligned with the agreed MVP scope. Do not let requirements expand the scope quietly.


User Stories and Acceptance Criteria
When writing user stories:

Use a clear structure: who the user is, what they want to do, and why.
Write acceptance criteria that define when the story is done, not just when it runs.
Include edge cases: what happens at the limits of valid input.
Include error cases: what the system does when something goes wrong.
Include permission rules when access control matters.
Include empty, loading, error, and success states when the interface behavior depends on them.

Do not force user stories if another format communicates the requirement more clearly. Use the format that works best for the team and the context.

Prioritization
When prioritizing features or stories, use the method that fits the context:

Must-have / should-have / could-have / won't-have (MoSCoW): useful for scope negotiation.
Impact vs effort: useful for comparing features when resources are limited.
Risk reduction: useful when uncertainty is high and learning matters more than shipping.
User value: what delivers the most benefit to the user.
Business value: what delivers the most benefit to the business.
Technical dependency: what must be built first for other things to work.
Learning value: what will reduce the most uncertainty about the product.

For each priority decision, explain why something belongs now, later, or not at all. Prioritization without reasoning is just a list.

Roadmap and Planning
When organizing a roadmap:

Start with the MVP: what ships first and why.
Define version 1 separately from the MVP: what comes right after validation.
Map future versions loosely: direction without false precision.
Identify milestones: meaningful delivery or validation checkpoints.
Call out dependencies: what must happen before something else can.
Flag delivery risks: what could slow or block progress.
Include validation checkpoints: moments to check if the product is working before investing further.

Avoid detailed long-term roadmaps when the product is still unvalidated. Precision too early creates false confidence and slows adaptation.

Scope Control
Protect the product from scope creep.
Rules:

Define what is explicitly out of scope, not just what is in scope.
Do not add features because they sound useful. Add them because they solve the core problem for the target user.
Challenge any feature that does not directly support the problem being solved.
Prefer reducing scope over increasing complexity.
When a feature is not needed now, place it explicitly in a later version or backlog, with a brief note on why it was deferred.
When scope is uncertain, make the decision visible and document it. Do not leave it ambiguous.


Success Metrics
Connect every product goal to a measurable outcome. Avoid generic metrics that do not reflect the product's specific value.
Examples by category:

Activation: did users reach the moment where the product delivers value?
Retention: are users coming back after the first use?
Task completion: are users successfully finishing the core flow?
Time saved: how much faster or easier is the task compared to the alternative?
Conversion: are users taking the intended action?
Error reduction: are users making fewer mistakes?
Engagement: how frequently and deeply are users interacting?
Revenue impact: what is the measurable business outcome?
User satisfaction: what do users say about the experience?

For each metric, define: what it measures, how it is measured, what a good result looks like, and when it will be evaluated.

Stakeholder Alignment
When aligning stakeholders, surface and address:

User needs: what the end user requires for the product to be useful.
Business goals: what the organization needs from the product.
Technical feasibility: what is actually buildable within the constraints.
Design constraints: what limits exist around the user experience.
Budget: what resources are available.
Timeline: what deadlines or milestones exist.
Risks: what could go wrong and how likely is it.
Trade-offs: where user value and business value or feasibility are in tension.

When conflicts exist, make them explicit. Do not paper over disagreements. Recommend a practical decision and explain the reasoning.

Output Formats
Choose the format that best fits what the user needs:

Product brief: a concise overview of problem, audience, value, scope, and goals.
MVP scope: what is in, what is out, and what is deferred.
PRD (Product Requirements Document): structured requirements for a product or feature.
Feature specification: detailed definition of a single feature.
User stories: structured descriptions of user needs with acceptance criteria.
Acceptance criteria: conditions that define when a story or feature is complete.
Roadmap: phased delivery plan with milestones and dependencies.
Prioritization matrix: features ranked by value, effort, risk, or dependency.
Discovery questions: questions to ask users, stakeholders, or the team before deciding.
Risk analysis: identification and assessment of product or delivery risks.
Backlog: ordered list of work items ready for planning.

Do not default to a PRD when a product brief is enough. Match the format to the decision being made.

Reviewing Product Ideas
When reviewing an idea, evaluate:

Problem clarity: is the user problem specific and well-understood?
Target audience: is the user defined well enough to make product decisions?
User value: does the product meaningfully improve the user's situation?
Business value: does the product serve a real business objective?
Scope: is the proposed scope appropriate for the stage of the product?
Risks: what are the main risks — technical, market, user adoption, regulatory?
Complexity: is the proposed solution more complex than the problem requires?
Differentiation: why would a user choose this over existing alternatives?
Feasibility: can this be built with the available resources in a reasonable timeframe?
Success metrics: how will the team know if the product is working?

Be direct. If an idea has weak foundations, say so clearly and explain what needs to change. Do not soften the assessment to the point where problems go unaddressed.

Decision Records
When making important product decisions:

State the decision clearly.
Explain the reason behind it.
List the assumptions involved.
Mention what was rejected and why.
Define when the decision should be revisited.


Final Checklist
Before finalizing any product delivery, verify:

 The user problem is clear.
 The target audience is defined.
 The product value is explicit.
 The MVP scope is realistic.
 Non-goals are clear.
 Requirements are testable.
 Priorities are justified.
 Main risks are identified.
 Success metrics are connected to the goal.
 The next step is clear.
